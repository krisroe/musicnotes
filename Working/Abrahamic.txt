Judaism
Sacred text: Tanakh (Hebrew Bible) — includes the Torah (first 5 books), Prophets (Nevi'im), and Writings (Ketuvim)
Scripture language: Biblical Hebrew, later Aramaic commentaries and Talmud in Rabbinic tradition
Holy site: Jerusalem is site of the First and Second Temples, and modern focal point of prayer
Calendar: Hebrew calendar
Passover: Celebrated with symbolic rituals (Seder meal, matzah)
Synagogues vs Sanctuaries: Synagogues used worldwide; no temple since 70 CE
Rabbinic Authority: Centralized — Torah interpreted through Talmud and rabbinic tradition
Priesthood: Priests (Kohanim) have a role, but Temple-based priesthood is not active
Self-identity: Descendants of all twelve tribes, focused on Judah and Levi; Jews
Samaritans often seen historically as heretics or schismatics (e.g., in the Talmud or New Testament)
Messiah: Belief in a future Messiah (varies by denomination)

Samaritanism
Sacred Text: Samaritan Pentateuch — only the first 5 books of Moses, with textual differences from the Masoretic (Jewish) version
Scripture language: Samaritan Hebrew, a script and dialect distinct from Jewish Hebrew
Holy site: Mount Gerizim is the true holy site, believed to be where God chose for His Temple (near modern Nablus, biblical Shechem)
Calendar: Samaritan calendar
Passover: Still includes actual lamb sacrifice on Mount Gerizim, as described in the Torah
Synagogues vs Sanctuaries: Central sanctuary on Mount Gerizim; small Samaritan synagogues exist too
Rabbinic Authority: no Talmud; direct interpretation of Torah without Rabbinic additions
Priesthood: Active hereditary priesthood — high priest from line of Aaron still officiates
Self-identity: See themselves as true Israelites, especially from tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh
View Jews as having strayed by accepting false prophets and Jerusalem as a false temple site
Messiah: Await a Taheb, a restorer figure like Moses who will rebuild the sanctuary on Mount Gerizim

Some Dead Sea Scrolls match readings in the Samaritan Pentateuch more than the Masoretic Text — suggesting that the SP preserves some ancient variants that were once more widespread.

Historical Divergence:
The split likely solidified during or after the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE).
Tensions during Second Temple period were high — visible in the New Testament (e.g., Good Samaritan, Woman at the Well stories).
Samaritans were often persecuted or marginalized by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike in later periods.

Samaritan timeline:
Biblical era (~13th–12th c. BCE)	Traditional beginning of priesthood with Aaron in wilderness period
Post-Solomon split (~930 BCE)	Northern Kingdom of Israel separates; Samaritans develop distinct religious identity
5th–4th c. BCE	Strong evidence of a rival temple on Mount Gerizim with functioning priesthood
2nd c. BCE (Maccabean period)	Jewish Hasmoneans destroy Gerizim temple, but priesthood continues in local form
Roman period	Josephus and early Christian sources mention active Samaritan priesthood
Modern era	The lineage is claimed to have persisted through persecutions, with high priests continuously appointed from priestly lines

Scholars cannot fully verify the unbroken continuity to Aaron due to the lack of external records prior to the Persian or Hellenistic period, but:
- The Samaritan Pentateuch and chronicles (e.g., Tulida, their genealogical book) preserve these records internally.
- Ancient Jewish and Christian sources confirm the existence and priestly functions of Samaritans dating back at least to the 5th century BCE.

Modern: two communities
Kiryat Luza (Mount Gerizim (West Bank)) Dual Israeli and Palestinian citizenship
Holon (near Tel Aviv) Israeli citizens

Jewish Temples:
1. First Temple (Soloman's Temple)
Built: ~957 BCE by King Solomon, son of David (1 Kings 6–8)
Location: Temple Mount, Jerusalem
Function: Central place of worship and sacrifice; housed the Ark of the Covenant
Significance: Fulfilled Davidic dream; symbol of God’s presence among Israelites
Destroyed: 586 BCE by the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar II
Aftermath: Led to the Babylonian Exile; start of synagogue worship traditions
2. Second Temple
Built: ~516 BCE after return from Babylonian exile under Zerubbabel, with Persian permission
Restored/Expanded: Significantly expanded by Herod the Great starting ~20 BCE
Function: Continuation of sacrificial system; center of Jewish worship and pilgrimage
Features: No Ark of the Covenant; inner sanctum (Holy of Holies) still central
Destroyed: 70 CE by the Romans under Titus, during the First Jewish-Roman War
Aftermath: Major turning point in Jewish history; ended priestly sacrifices; rise of Rabbinic Judaism and synagogue-based worship
3. Third Temple (Hoped-For/Future Temple)
Status: Not yet built; central in Jewish eschatology and Messianic expectations
Location: Expected to be built on the Temple Mount, where Al-Aqsa Mosque and Dome of the Rock now stand
Obstacle: Site is a flashpoint in Israeli-Palestinian tensions; under control of Islamic Waqf
Religious Groups: Orthodox Jews generally pray for its future; some fringe groups support building it now
Christian Views: Some evangelical Christians also expect a Third Temple as part of end-times prophecy (see Revelation, Daniel)
Islamic Concerns: Many Muslims see efforts to rebuild as a threat to Al-Aqsa and Islamic heritage

Temple Mount Today
- The Temple Mount remains a sacred site for Jews, Muslims, and Christians.
- Jews pray at the Western Wall, the last remnant of the Second Temple retaining wall.
- The Al-Aqsa Mosque and Dome of the Rock dominate the mount today, making it one of the most religiously and politically sensitive places in the world.
- Ariel Sharon's visit (2000-09-28) sparked the Second Intifada (Al-Aqsa Intifada)

Judaism
Original Sin: Not a dominant doctrine; humans are born innocent and responsible for their own sins
Law and Grace: Law is a gift, providing guidance and closeness to God
Chosen People: Jews are uniquely chosen through Abraham’s lineage and Torah observance
Genesis 22 - Binding of Isaac: Known as the Akedah (binding); emphasizes Abraham’s obedience and Isaac's survival
Exodus - Passover: Celebration of God’s deliverance from Egypt; focus on Jewish identity and history
Leviticus - Sacrificial System: Foundation of temple worship; seen as symbolic after 70 CE

Christianity
Original Sin: Genesis 3 (Adam and Eve) is read as the Fall, affecting all humanity
Law and Grace: Emphasis on grace over law, with Paul contrasting the two (especially in Romans and Galatians)
Chosen People: Some Christian traditions see the Church as the "new Israel", expanding chosenness
Genesis 22 - Binding of Isaac: Seen as a foreshadowing of Jesus’ sacrifice, with Isaac as a "type" of Christ
Exodus - Passover: Jesus is seen as the Paschal Lamb; the Last Supper is a new Passover fulfilled in Christ
Leviticus - Sacrificial System: Understood as a prefiguration of Christ’s atonement (esp. in Hebrews 9–10)

Adam & Eve


Judaism
Scripture: Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)
Creation: God creates Adam from dust and Eve from his side/rib (Genesis 2)
Names: Adam and Eve (Chava)
Setting: Garden of Eden
Prohibition: Don’t eat from Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil
Serpent/Satan: Serpent is not equated with Satan
Temptation: Serpent tempts Eve, who gives to Adam
Consequence: Expelled from the Garden; labor, pain, mortality introduced
Original Sin: No doctrine of inherited sin
Guilt/Blame: Sin is personal; each person is accountable
Role of Eve: Not singled out for greater blame
Salvation/Redemption: Achieved through repentance, good deeds, and Torah

Christianity
Scripture: Old Testament
Creation: Same as Judaism (Genesis 2–3); often read as literal or symbolic
Names: Adam and Eve
Setting: Garden of Eden
Prohibition: Don’t eat from Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil
Serpent/Satan: Serpent is often interpreted as Satan
Temptation: Often interpreted as Satan speaking through the serpent
Consequence: Same, but with added theological interpretation (Original Sin)
Original Sin: Yes — Adam’s sin introduces a fallen state passed on to all humanity
Guilt/Blame: Humanity inherits sin; Jesus needed to atone for it
Role of Eve: Often viewed as the one who led Adam astray; historically blamed more
Salvation/Redemption: Through faith in Jesus Christ, who redeems humanity from sin

Islam
Scripture: Not part of the Qur’an, but similar story appears in Surahs 2, 7, 20
Creation: Adam created from clay; Eve not named in the Qur’an but created from Adam (Surah 4:1)
Names: Adam and his wife (unnamed in Qur’an, but called Hawwa in later tradition)
Setting: Garden, sometimes called Jannah (Paradise), but not necessarily Heaven
Prohibition: Don’t approach the Tree (unspecified type)
Serpent/Satan: Iblis (Satan) tempts them directly
Temptation: Satan (Iblis) tempts both Adam and his wife
Consequence: Expelled from the Garden; forgiven after repentance; no Original Sin
Original Sin: No — Adam and Eve sinned but were forgiven, and humans are born sinless
Guilt/Blame: Sin is personal; no inherited guilt
Role of Eve: Both Adam and his wife are equally responsible
Salvation/Redemption: Through repentance and submission to God (Islam means submission)
