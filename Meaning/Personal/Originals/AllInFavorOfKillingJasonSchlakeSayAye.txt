Meaning: Jason Schlake was a classmate of my sister's in high school (two years younger than me). I never spoke to him directly, my only connection to him was that my sister and he were in the percussion section of high school band when I was a senior and they were sophomores. He wasn't particularly flashy or showy and despite or because of this I wrote several original songs about him. This was the last one, and is the longest and most complex of those songs. This isn't really an insult song, it creates an alternate reality where the target of the song is destined to destroy the world, presenting problems similar to a thought experiment where if you go back in time and have the ability to kill Hitler would you do it. 

The man at the start is a stereotyped religious zealot holding a sign that is an omen of the end of the world. Typically you'd expect such a figure to urge repentance or religious conversion, but here he thinks the world is fine, other than a possibility the target of the song (a publicly unknown person) should be destroyed to tie up a loose end where he might end up the master of the universe (presumably bad).

The chorus of the song sets up a vote where people can choose to either kill or spare the target of the song. This is ridiculous, because the target hasn't done anything wrong (yet), people don't really know enough about the target to make an informed decision, and voting to execute someone is only a thing in capital punishment scenarios.

Nevertheless, the song continues by enlisting a random bystander. The random bystander is informed of the facts (not given in the song). And the random bystander finds it loud and clear that killing Jason Schlake is the appropriate course of action. Perhaps one could think that there are secret facts in play influencing the decision, but the song as written does not envision that the decision is rational.

The last verse of the song sets up a meeting with Jason Schlake himself, who denies whatever allegations there are against him. He suggests putting the matter to a vote, presumably assuming that there's no way it could possibly go against him. But the result is not only in favor of killing, it seems to be unanimous, with the question of whether to spare him responded to with silence.

If there's a practical message in the song, I think it's an over-the-top way of describing human voting processes as rational. Humans are subject to various cognitive biases that shape their voting decisions, such as thinking they are more informed that they actually are and interpreting new information as confirming biases. The song is too ridiculous to seriously discuss such matters, so the song itself stands as a bizarre targeting of an unknown person for execution for unknown reasons. But that's good enough for me, it's my song.
