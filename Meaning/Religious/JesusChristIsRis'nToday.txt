Jesus Christ is ris'n today (Christian Easter Hymn)

Jesus Christ is ris'n today. (Alleluia! follows each line)
Our triumphant holy day.
Died for us upon the cross.
Suffered to redeem our loss.

Hymns of praise, it's time to sing.
Unto Christ, our beloved king.
Who steadfastly bore the grave.
Sinners to forgive and save.

Thru the pains which He endured.
Our salvation is procured.
He's beyond the sky, divine.
Where souls and angels ever shine.

Our brave Lord now reigns above.
Life eternal, as His love.
Praise Him well, ye heav'nly host.
Father, Son, and Holy Ghost.

Analysis (with assistance from ChatGPT)

Verse 1 (Resurrection and redemption)
The first line establishes the central paradox of Easter: an event involving death and suffering becomes a celebration of triumph.
"Our triumphant holy day" emphasizes the celebration itself, rather than simply describing Christ's resurrection. The possessive "our"
also places the congregation inside the event, making Easter a shared human experience. The third line shifts immediately from
resurrection to crucifixion, explaining the suffering that preceded it. The loss that is redeemed is the humanity's sinful nature dating
back to Adam and Eve eating the apple from the forbidden tree of knowledge.

Verse 2 (Praise and perseverance)
This verse moves from describing the Easter event to prescribing a human response: singing praise. "It's time to sing" is more
conversational than traditional hymn language. It creates a sense of immediacy, almost as though the singer is inviting everyone present
to participate. "Our beloved king" balances two aspects of Christ: royal authority and personal affection. "King" emphasizes power,
while "bellowed" emphasizes an emotional relationship. "He steadfastly bore the grave." is a distinctive phrase, where "steadfastly" implies
determination, constancy, and an unwillingness to abandon one's purpose. It portrays Christ's suffering not merely as something that happened
to him, but as something he willingly endured. "Bore the grave" is an interesting construction. People normally bear burdens, suffering, or
grief rather than graves. Metaphorically, however, it suggests Christ bearing the weight of death itself. The phrase "sinners to forgive and
save" presents two related but distinct concepts: "Forgive" addresses moral wrongdoing and guilt. "Save" addresses the ultimate consequences
of sin and death. "He steadfastly bore the grave. Sinners to forgive and save." is not ordinary conversational syntax, but the interpretation
is clear enough in hymn language.

Verse 3 (Suffering, salvation, and heaven)
The first two lines express a clear causal relationship: Christ endured suffering, and through that suffering, salvation was obtained. "Procured"
doesn't necessarily assert that every human being is saved. It leaves room for different theological positions about who ultimately receives
redemption. "Procured" also has a slightly archaic quality that complements the hymn's traditional language. One subtle consideration is that
"procured" can evoke a transaction, as though salvation were purchased, but that is consistent with longstanding Christian language about
redemption. "He's beyond the sky, divine" establishes a transition from Christ's historical suffering to his present heavenly existence. The word
"divine" interprets naturally as describing Christ, although its placement still permits a secondary association with the heavenly realm. "Where
souls and angels ever shine" asserts that some but not necessarily all souls inhabit heaven. "Ever shine" invokes an image of eternal radiance. It
suggests that heaven is not merely a place of continued existence but one of enduring spiritual glory. In total, the third verse progresses from
suffering ("pains") to glory ("shine").

Verse 4 (Sovereignty, eternity, and praise)
"Brave" is not among the most conventional adjectives used to describe Christ in Easter hymns. Worlds such as "glorious" and "mighty" are more 
typical. But "brave" connects directly to the suffering described in verse 3. Christ is not simply powerful; he has demonstrated courage in
facing death. That gives his subsequent reign a particular significance: the one who reigns is also the one who suffered. "Life eternal, as His love"
connects the eternity of heavenly life with the eternity of divine love, implying life is eternal just as God's love is eternal. "Praise Him well"
suggests that praise should be worthy of its recipient. It isn't merely a command to praise Christ. It asks the heavenly host to praise him properly,
fully, or excellently. The final line "Father, Son, and Holy Ghost" provides a traditional Trinitarian conclusion.
